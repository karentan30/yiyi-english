10 Tips for Parents: How to Make Online English Classes More Effective

Online English lessons are now the standard for millions of Chinese families — and for good reason. The best teachers in the world are accessible without a commute, at flexible hours, and at a fraction of the cost of in-person centres. But the format also introduces unique challenges that in-person classes don't have: technical glitches, shorter attention spans, the temptation of other devices, and the absence of a physical classroom environment that signals "now we learn."

Parents play a much larger role in online learning success than most realise. Research on home learning environments consistently shows that parental setup, involvement, and follow-up account for a significant portion of the variance in children's academic progress. Here are 10 specific, actionable things you can do to dramatically improve the return on every lesson.

Tip 1: Build a Dedicated English Learning Space

Your child's brain is associative. The same chair they sit in to watch cartoons will not naturally activate "focus mode." The ideal is a dedicated small desk or corner used only for English learning — not homework in other subjects, not iPad time, not meals. Even in a small apartment, a specific spot with a few consistent objects (their English books, a small whiteboard, a favourite toy they "teach" English to) creates a context cue that says: this is where we do English.

If a separate space isn't possible, a consistent visual ritual works almost as well: put on a specific lamp, clear the desk completely, place their English notebook in front of them, and play 2 minutes of an English song. After a few weeks, these actions themselves signal "English time" to the brain.

Tip 2: Sort the Audio Setup Before Day One

Poor audio is the number one technical factor that degrades online lesson quality — and it's almost entirely preventable. Built-in laptop microphones pick up ambient room noise, echo from walls, and the hum of air conditioners. The teacher ends up repeatedly saying "sorry, can you say that again?" — which kills lesson momentum and discourages speaking.

Invest in a basic headset (budget: ¥80–150) with a boom microphone positioned close to the mouth. For children aged 5–8, over-ear headphones with a built-in microphone are easiest. For older children, an in-ear set is fine. A wired headset is more reliable than Bluetooth for avoiding audio dropouts. This single purchase will improve every lesson for the entire time your child takes online classes.

Camera-wise: position the camera at eye level (a stack of books under the laptop works fine) so the teacher sees your child's face rather than the top of their head. Natural or warm artificial lighting from the front of the face, not behind, ensures the teacher can read your child's expressions clearly — important for a good teacher reading engagement and comprehension.

Tip 3: Do a 5-Minute English Warm-Up Before Class

Walking straight from dinner table to an English lesson cold produces the equivalent of running a sprint without stretching. Your child's English "channel" — the mental mode of processing and producing English — needs activating. A 5-minute warm-up bridges the gap between their Chinese-language day and the English lesson.

Simple warm-up options: watch one episode of a short English cartoon (Bluey, Peppa Pig, or Daniel Tiger for younger children). Listen to one English song in the car on the way back from school. Ask your child 3 questions in English: "What did you have for lunch? What was the best part of your day? What are you looking forward to in class today?" You don't need perfect answers — the point is to activate the English channel.

Tip 4: Remove All Other Screens From the Room

This one is simple but frequently ignored. If your child's iPad, Nintendo Switch, or sibling's phone is visible in the room during class, it will draw their attention — repeatedly. Children's attention systems are not yet fully developed; the pull of familiar entertainment is simply stronger than the pull of cognitive effort. Put devices in another room, or in a drawer, before class begins. No exceptions.

The Two-Device Rule: The only device in the room during class should be the one running the lesson. If your child needs to look at lesson materials on a tablet, position it directly next to the laptop so teacher and materials are in the same visual field.

Tip 5: Review Last Session's Notes in the 10 Minutes Before Class

One of the biggest wastes in language tutoring is the first 5–10 minutes of each lesson spent re-teaching vocabulary and concepts from the previous session that the student has forgotten. This is not the teacher's fault — it's a natural result of the way memory works if material isn't reviewed between sessions.

The spaced repetition principle (see our plateau article) tells us that reviewing material within 24 hours of learning dramatically increases retention. Before the next class, spend 5–10 minutes looking through last session's notes, vocabulary list, or any materials the teacher sent. Have your child recite 5 words or retell one story from the previous lesson. This simple habit can recover 10–15 minutes of learning time per lesson by reducing re-teaching.

Tip 6: Know When to Support and When to Step Back

Many parents make one of two mistakes: either hovering anxiously nearby — interjecting, answering for the child, correcting mid-sentence — or disappearing completely and leaving young children unsupported with technology. Both extremes undermine learning.

For children under 7: sit nearby but out of the camera frame. Be available for technical issues, bathroom breaks, or major emotional moments — but stay silent. Don't coach through gestures. Let the teacher do their job. Your child needs to develop the habit of communicating independently with the teacher.

For children 8 and older: leave the room. Children perform significantly better speaking English when they're not self-conscious about a parent hearing their mistakes. Tell them: "I'm in the next room if you need me. I'm proud of you for working independently." Then actually leave.

Tip 7: Debrief Within 30 Minutes After Class

The 30 minutes immediately after a lesson are the highest-value consolidation window. The material is fresh, the emotional experience of the lesson is still vivid, and the brain is primed to encode new information into long-term memory. Don't waste this window by immediately moving to dinner or screen time.

A 10-minute post-class debrief achieves more consolidation than 1 hour of review two days later. Ask your child:

  • "What was the most interesting thing you learned today?"
  • "What was hard? What are you going to practise?"
  • "Can you teach me one new word in English?"
  • "What's the story you read about?" (Have them retell it in English — even haltingly)

The act of retrieving and expressing new information strengthens memory traces far more effectively than passive re-reading of notes.

Tip 8: Keep a Shared Vocabulary Notebook

Ask the teacher to send 5–10 new vocabulary items after each lesson. Keep a shared paper notebook where your child writes each word, its meaning, and a drawing or example sentence. Not a phone app — a physical notebook. Research on handwriting and memory (Mueller and Oppenheimer, 2014) consistently shows that hand-writing notes produces better long-term retention than typing because it requires processing and paraphrasing rather than verbatim copying.

Make the notebook a source of pride. Add stickers. Put the date. Review old pages together periodically. After 6 months, a filled vocabulary notebook is tangible evidence of progress — something children can see and feel proud of.

Tip 9: Communicate With the Teacher Like a Co-Coach

The best parents of English learners treat the teacher as a professional partner, not a service provider. After each lesson, send a brief message: "He seemed much more confident talking about animals today — thank you for making it fun! He's still struggling with the 'th' sound — any exercises we can do at home?" This kind of communication helps the teacher calibrate future lessons and helps you reinforce learning at home.

Share relevant information proactively: upcoming school events that might disrupt the lesson schedule, topics your child is passionate about (dinosaurs, football, BTS), concerns about progress, and anything emotionally significant at home that might be affecting focus. Teachers who understand the whole child teach better.

Tip 10: Prioritise Consistency Over Intensity

The most common mistake in online English tutoring is the "sprint and crash" pattern: a family books 5 lessons per week during a school holiday, the child makes rapid progress, then school starts and lessons drop to once every two weeks. The intensive period's gains are largely lost within 4–6 weeks of reduced practice.

Language acquisition is a long game built on consistent, regular exposure. Two lessons per week, every week, for 2 years will produce vastly better results than 5 lessons per week for 2 months followed by 6 months of nothing. Set a schedule you can maintain even during busy school terms — even one lesson per week sustained over 3 years is a serious investment.

Set the lesson time at the same day and hour each week. Make it as non-negotiable as a sport practice or music lesson. The routine itself signals to your child that English is a long-term priority in your family — not a passing enthusiasm.

Progress Tracking Idea: Every 3 months, record your child speaking English for 2–3 minutes about a topic they like. Date the recording and save it. Six months later, play the old recording side by side with a new one. The improvement is often striking and deeply motivating for children to hear — they can hear themselves getting better.
