7 Red Flags When Choosing an Online English Tutor

Chinese parents spend enormous time and money on English tutoring. But the online tutoring market is largely unregulated, and it's remarkably easy to pay for 200 hours of lessons that produce almost no real progress. The tutors who seem most enthusiastic in a trial class are not always the tutors who actually teach well.

Here are seven specific warning signs — based on years of experience in English education — that should make you pause before signing any tutoring contract.

Red Flag #1

No Teaching Certification — Or Vague Credentials

Being a native English speaker is not a qualification. It's where you were born. A qualified English teacher should hold at minimum a TEFL (Teaching English as a Foreign Language), TESOL, or CELTA certificate, or a state/national teaching license from their home country.

Ask directly: "Can you show me your teaching certificate?" A legitimate tutor will share this immediately. If the answer is "I'm a native speaker and I've been teaching for years," that's not an answer — that's a deflection.

Also watch for: Tutors who claim degrees but can't name the university, or who list "BA in English" without any teaching credential. A degree in literature is not teacher training.

Red Flag #2

They Can't Explain Grammar Rules

During a trial class, ask the tutor to explain why Chinese learners often use "he" and "she" interchangeably in English (it's because Mandarin's third-person pronoun tā is gender-neutral). Can they explain it clearly? Can they suggest how to fix it?

Or ask: "Why is 'I am very agree' wrong, but 'I strongly agree' is correct?" A qualified teacher explains that "agree" is a verb, not an adjective, and that adverbs modify verbs. An unqualified teacher says "it just sounds wrong."

The test: Ask one grammar question that goes beyond the obvious. If the tutor can only model correct usage but can't explain the rule, your child will repeat the same mistakes forever.

Red Flag #3

Every Lesson is "Free Talk"

Conversation practice is valuable. An entire lesson plan built around "let's just chat" is not teaching — it's companionship. Free talk does not systematically build grammar, expand vocabulary, or develop writing skills.

Strong lessons for children should include a clear learning objective ("today we're practicing past continuous tense"), a structured activity, targeted error correction, and a brief review of what was learned. This doesn't mean every moment is rigid — but there should be a plan.

Ask before booking: "What's your lesson structure for a child at my child's level?" If the answer is vague, walk away.

Red Flag #4

Inconsistent Teachers

Some platforms assign a different teacher to every class. This might seem like variety, but for children — especially ages 5–12 — relationship with the teacher is the single biggest driver of motivation. Children who feel connected to their teacher attend more, participate more, and retain more.

Rotating teachers also means no teacher ever knows where your child is struggling. Each lesson starts from scratch with a new person reviewing the same basics instead of building on previous sessions.

The standard: Your child should have one primary teacher for at least 80% of lessons. Occasional substitutes are unavoidable — a permanent rotation is a structural flaw.

Red Flag #5

No Feedback System or Progress Reports

After each lesson, parents should receive something — even a brief note — about what was covered, what the child did well, and what needs more work. If the tutor or platform provides nothing, you have no way to know whether your money is being well spent.

The minimum acceptable feedback: a post-lesson summary with 2–3 specific observations about your child's performance. Better tutors provide monthly progress reports against stated goals.

Ask in the trial: "How do you communicate with parents about progress?" If the answer is "you can ask me anytime," that means they don't — and you'll need to chase them.

Red Flag #6

The Price is Suspiciously Low

Quality English teaching from a qualified teacher with experience in youth education has a real market rate. In 2026, that's typically USD $25–60 per hour depending on qualifications, experience, and platform overhead.

Tutors charging USD $8–12 per hour for "native speaker" lessons are almost always cutting costs somewhere: no teaching credentials, no lesson preparation time, shared students, or sessions in low-concentration environments.

The math: If a tutor is charging very little per hour, they need very many students to earn a reasonable income. That means minimal preparation time per student, minimal lesson customization, and no margin to care about individual progress.

Red Flag #7

Vague or Non-Existent Curriculum

A legitimate tutoring service should be able to show you — in writing — what your child will learn over the next 3, 6, and 12 months. This curriculum should reference recognized frameworks such as the Cambridge English scale (A1 to C2), the Common European Framework (CEFR), or a specific exam preparation track (IELTS, SSAT, Cambridge Young Learners).

If a tutor says "I tailor everything to your child individually" but cannot tell you what level your child will reach in 6 months or what skills they'll have, that's not personalization — it's the absence of a plan.

What Good Looks Like: A Checklist

Before signing up for any tutoring program, you should be able to answer "yes" to all of these:

✓ The tutor has a named, verifiable teaching certification (TEFL/TESOL/CELTA or equivalent)
✓ The tutor has documented experience teaching children in your child's age group
✓ You've seen a sample lesson plan that includes a clear learning objective
✓ The same teacher will teach your child consistently (80%+ of sessions)
✓ There's a written progress report system, at minimum monthly
✓ The curriculum references an international standard (CEFR, Cambridge, IELTS Junior)
✓ The price reflects professional teaching rates, not a race to the bottom
One more check: After the trial class, ask your child: "Did the teacher explain things clearly? Did you feel like you learned something new?" Children are surprisingly good at identifying whether they've been taught well or just entertained.
