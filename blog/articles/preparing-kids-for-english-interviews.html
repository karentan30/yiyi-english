How to Prepare Your Child for English School Interviews

The English interview is one of the highest-stakes moments in a child's education journey — whether they're applying to an international school in Shanghai, a UK boarding school at 13+, or a prestigious high school in the United States. And yet most families prepare for it the wrong way: scripting perfect answers instead of building genuine communication ability.

This guide explains exactly what schools are testing, gives you real practice questions by age, and shows you how to build the confidence that actually impresses interviewers.

What Schools Are Actually Testing

Experienced admissions interviewers are not checking whether your child has memorized perfect sentences. They are assessing three core qualities:

  1. Communication confidence: Can the child maintain eye contact, speak at a natural pace, and handle unexpected questions without shutting down?
  2. Curiosity and intellectual engagement: Does the child show genuine interest in ideas? Can they elaborate beyond a one-word answer?
  3. English fluency under pressure: Is their English functional in a real conversation, not just a rehearsed monologue?

Many Chinese students fail interviews not because their English is weak — but because they freeze when asked something they didn't prepare for. A child who says "That's an interesting question. I've never thought about it that way, but I think..." scores much higher than a child who goes silent.

Interview Formats by School Type

International Schools (Grades 1–8 Entry)

Usually 15–30 minutes, one-on-one with a teacher or admissions officer. Focus is on personality, interests, and basic English communication. Some schools include a short reading task or picture description.

UK 13+ (Common Entrance / Senior School Entry)

More structured. Often includes a topic card — the child reads a short passage and is asked to discuss it. Schools like Eton, Harrow, and Wycombe Abbey want evidence of independent thinking, not just fluency.

US High School / Boarding School (SSAT + Interview)

Typically 30–45 minutes. Conversational but probing. Interviewers may ask about current events, books read recently, or hypothetical scenarios. The interview often feels like a friendly chat — which is exactly when unprepared students relax too much and say nothing interesting.

Practice Questions by Age Group

Ages 6–9 (Kindergarten / Primary Entry)

Common Questions
  • "What is your favorite thing to do after school?"
  • "Tell me about your best friend."
  • "If you had a superpower, what would it be and why?"
  • "What book have you read recently?"
  • "What makes you laugh?"

What to practice: Full sentences (not "football" but "I like playing football because..."), looking at the interviewer while speaking, and not rushing to finish.

Ages 10–12 (Middle School Entry)

Common Questions
  • "What has been the biggest challenge you've faced, and how did you handle it?"
  • "If you could change one thing about your school, what would it be?"
  • "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a friend. What happened?"
  • "What subject do you find most difficult? What do you do when you're stuck?"
  • "Describe a person you admire and explain why."

What to practice: The STAR structure — Situation, Task, Action, Result. Even at this age, structured storytelling impresses interviewers significantly more than simple lists of facts.

Ages 13–16 (Senior/High School Entry)

Common Questions
  • "What's a current news story that interests you? What's your opinion on it?"
  • "If you could have dinner with any historical figure, who would you choose and why?"
  • "Describe a time you failed at something. What did you learn?"
  • "How do you think artificial intelligence will change education?"
  • "Why do you want to come to this school specifically?" (Research the school thoroughly.)

The Five Biggest Mistakes Chinese Students Make

  1. Over-scripting. Parents write out perfect answers and children memorize them. This is detectable within 30 seconds. Interviewers ask one follow-up question and the whole script collapses.
  2. Speaking too quietly. Many Chinese children are taught that quietness is polite. In a Western interview context, a quiet voice reads as lack of confidence. Practice speaking at 20% louder than feels natural.
  3. Not asking any questions. Every interview has a moment where the child is invited to ask questions. Having two or three thoughtful questions prepared shows genuine interest and leaves a strong final impression.
  4. Using Chinese filler habits in English. Long pauses of silence while thinking feel uncomfortable. Train children to use English thinking phrases: "That's a great question..." or "Let me think about that for a moment..."
  5. Answering only what's asked. Strong candidates elaborate voluntarily. After answering, they add: "What I find particularly interesting about this is..." This is the difference between a conversation and a quiz.
Body language checklist: Sit up straight (but relaxed), hands on lap or the table, maintain eye contact 70% of the time (not 100% — that's staring), nod occasionally when listening, and smile genuinely. These are all trainable with practice.

A 6-Week Mock Interview Plan

Use this schedule for children applying 8–10 weeks before their interview date:

  • Weeks 1–2: Warm-up questions only (hobbies, family, school). Focus on full sentences and eye contact. Record videos — this feedback is more powerful than any coaching.
  • Weeks 3–4: Deeper personal questions (challenges, achievements, people you admire). Introduce the STAR method. Practice follow-up questions from the interviewer.
  • Week 5: Full mock interview (30 minutes, timed, unknown questions). Debrief immediately after with specific, constructive feedback.
  • Week 6: Light maintenance — one or two short sessions, focus on confidence not content. Over-drilling this week increases anxiety.
